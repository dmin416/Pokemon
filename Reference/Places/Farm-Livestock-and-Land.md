# Feeding 40 People from One Farm: Livestock and Land Requirements

Planning benchmarks for a self-sufficient temperate farm (D Ranch scale). Pair with [Paldea-Farm-Climate.md](Paldea-Farm-Climate.md), [Iberian-Land-Values.md](Iberian-Land-Values.md), and [Growth-and-Season.md](Growth-and-Season.md). Story ranch state: `Stories/Story 1/Story 1 References/Farm.md`.

## TL;DR

- **Milk potential:** A modern dairy cow at sustained commercial output averages ~24,000 lb (~2,800 gal / ~10,900 L) per 305-day lactation - roughly 7-8 gal (26-40 L) per day at peak, ~3-4 gal/day averaged over a homestead lactation; a top dairy goat (Saanen/Alpine) yields ~2,500-2,700 lb (~230-330 gal) per 284-day lactation, ~1-1.5 gal/day at peak.
- **Livestock for 40 people:** Plan for roughly **3-4 milking cows** (herd of ~6-8 head) or ~15-18 milking goats; **~55-70 laying hens**; and **~350-500 meat birds raised in batches per year** plus **~5-6 beef/dairy-beef animals finished per year** for a moderate-meat diet.
- **Land and produce:** Budget roughly **1.5-2.5 acres per person -> ~60-100 acres total** in a temperate climate - of which ~15-25 acres is intensive cropland (vegetables, staple grains, orchard) and the balance is pasture and hay/forage for the animals; grow ~20-30 vegetable/fruit crops plus calorie staples (potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, grain).

## Key Findings

### 1. Milk production potential

**Dairy cows.** The U.S. average dairy cow produced **24,177 pounds of milk in 2024**, per USDA Economic Research Service dairy data (up from 24,117 lb in 2023) - roughly 2,800 gallons, about 7.4 gallons (~28 L) per day averaged across the milking herd. Peak daily yield for a high Holstein runs 30-40 L/day (8-10+ gal); Jerseys peak lower (~19-25 L) but give richer, higher-butterfat milk. A standard lactation is defined as **305 days**, followed by a **~60-day dry period** (recommended 45-60 days), on a target **12-13-month calving interval**. Peak output occurs 4-8 weeks after calving, then declines; "continuous" milking is not literal - the cow must be re-bred and rested.

**Dairy goats.** Lactation averages **284 days**. Among the best dairy breeds, ADGA 2010 lactation averages for Saanen were **2,545 lb of milk** (3.2% fat, 2.8% protein), while the highest-producing DHIR-tested does can exceed **5,000 lb in a 305-day lactation**. Practically, top Saanen/Alpine does average roughly 1 gallon/day and up to 1.5-3 gal/day at peak in elite animals; Nubians give less volume (~2,000 lb) but the highest butterfat. A common homestead planning figure is ~233 gallons (~2,000 lb) per doe per year, at ~0.75-1.5 gal/day over the lactation. Roughly 6-8 good goats ≈ one average cow in milk volume.

### 2. Livestock for a 40-person farm

**Human requirements (baselines):**

- Energy: a 2,000-kcal/day diet is the standard reference; active farm laborers need more (~2,500-3,000 kcal/day).
- Protein: RDA ~0.8 g/kg body weight - ~46 g/day (women) to ~56 g/day (men), ~50 g/day rule-of-thumb.
- Dairy: U.S. per-capita consumption ≈ **651 lb on a milk-fat (milk-equivalent) basis in 2024** (USDA ERS), including ~127 lb fluid milk, ~40 lb natural cheese, ~6.5-6.8 lb butter.
- Eggs: **274 eggs per capita in 2024** (Congressional Research Service).
- Meat: U.S. availability ~**226-228 lb/person/year** (red meat + poultry, USDA); a more modest self-sufficient target is ~100-150 lb/person/year.

**Dairy animals.** Total dairy demand for 40 people ≈ 26,000 lb milk-equivalent/year. Options:

- **Cows:** A homestead family cow (Jersey) realistically gives 3-4 gal/day over lactation (~1,000-1,200 gal ≈ 8,600-10,300 lb per lactation). Three such cows cover the milk-equivalent demand; to guarantee year-round supply through dry periods, keep **3-4 milking cows on staggered calving** within a total herd of ~6-8 head (milkers, dry cows, replacement heifers, calves), plus a bull or AI. High-producing commercial-fed cows could cut milkers to ~2, but reduce resilience.
- **Goats (alternative):** ~13 does at 2,000 lb each meet demand; allowing for dry periods and variation -> **~15-18 milking does** in a herd of ~25-30 head.

**Laying hens.** 40 × 274 eggs ≈ 10,960 eggs/year. Commercial layers averaged **290 eggs/year in 2024** (USDA NASS), but backyard/homestead flocks realistically average ~180-220/year across a productive life (molt, winter light, aging), with high-production first-year birds reaching ~250. At ~200 eggs/hen -> ~55 hens; buffer for molt and replacement -> **~55-70 laying hens**, replaced on a ~2-3-year rotation.

**Meat.** For a moderate ~100-150 lb/person/year target (4,000-6,000 lb/year):

- **Beef/dairy-beef:** a 1,200-lb steer dresses ~62% -> ~450-490 lb of retail cuts. About **5-6 animals finished per year** cover the beef share; the dairy herd's own calves supply much of this.
- **Poultry meat:** a 6-lb broiler dresses ~72% (~4.3 lb carcass, ~3 lb meat). **~350-500 broilers/year**, raised in rotating batches of ~100, plus culled laying hens and surplus goat kids/calves.

### 3. Produce, crop diversity, and land

**Vegetables.** Per Ecology Action research, "one person can grow a nutritionally complete, plant-based diet on approximately 4,000 square feet - less than one-tenth of an acre - using biointensive methods at intermediate yield levels" (including compost/cover crops). For 40 people that's ~3.7 acres of very intensively managed beds; a more forgiving traditional-row garden needs ~0.2-0.25 acre/person (~8-10 acres for 40). Grow **20-30 crops across families**: leafy greens (kale, chard, lettuce, spinach), brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower), roots (carrots, beets, onions, turnips), nightshades (tomatoes, peppers, potatoes), legumes (beans, peas), and cucurbits (squash, cucumbers, melons, pumpkins).

**Calorie/staple crops.** These dominate real self-sufficiency:

- **Grain (wheat):** ~200 lb grain/person/year for bread (LDS storage standard ~300 lb for all grains). 40 people ≈ 8,000-12,000 lb. Homestead wheat yields ~2,000-3,000 lb/acre (USDA field average ~2,400-3,300 lb/acre) -> **~3-5 acres**.
- **Potatoes:** extremely calorie-dense; U.S. commercial yields ~45,000 lb/acre (454 cwt/acre in 2024), homestead ~15,000-20,000 lb/acre. A modest amount of land feeds many.
- **Dry beans, winter squash, sweet potatoes** for storable calories and protein.

**Typical temperate yields per acre** (planning figures): wheat ~2,400-3,300 lb; potatoes ~15,000-45,000 lb; cabbage ~13,700 lb; carrots ~19,400 lb; tomatoes ~11,000+ lb; onions ~19,800 lb.

**Pasture/forage - the largest land use.** A dairy cow needs ~2 acres of average pasture (1 animal unit ≈ 1,000 lb; dairy cow ≈ 1.3 AU), plus hay ground for winter. A cattle herd of ~6-8 head -> ~20-40 acres pasture/hay. Goats stock at ~5-8/acre; broilers can be rotated at ~400-800/acre. On marginal or unirrigated land, requirements rise sharply (15-18 acres/cow on dry rangeland).

**Total land.** Homesteading and FAO references span 2-17 acres/person depending on diet and inputs; a meat-inclusive, no-fossil-fuel diet runs ~5.8-9.4 acres/person. For 40 people with dairy, eggs, moderate meat, and produce in a good temperate climate with active management, budget **~60-100 acres total**: ~15-25 acres intensive cropland (vegetables + grain + orchard) and ~45-75 acres pasture and hay/forage.

## Details

**Milk conversions and continuous production.** Milk weighs ~8.6 lb/gallon. The 305-day/60-day-dry standard exists because cows are kept pregnant and re-freshened annually; lactations can be extended if milking continues, and >30% of modern cows already milk beyond 305 days, but yield per day falls and persistency varies. Dry periods of 40-70 days maximize the *next* lactation; very short (<40 d) or long (>70 d) dry periods reduce subsequent yield and raise health risks. Once-a-day milking (common on homesteads) cuts yield ~10-25% versus twice daily but suits family-scale needs. Useful conversion factors for planning: ~9.2 lb of farm milk per lb of American cheese, ~21.8 lb of milk per lb of butter (milkfat basis).

**Why staggered breeding matters.** Because each animal has a dry period, a farm relying on a single cow or a single kidding date will have milk-less months. Keeping ≥2-3 cows (or several does) on offset calving/kidding schedules smooths year-round supply - the reason homesteaders keep at least two milking does.

**Meat integration with the dairy herd.** A self-sufficient system is circular: dairy cows calve annually, and surplus (especially bull) calves are raised for beef; culled cows and does become meat; goat kids and spent laying hens add to the meat supply. This substantially reduces the number of dedicated meat animals needed.

**Nutritional balance.** Calorie-dense storable crops (potatoes, grain, winter squash, dry beans) are the backbone of winter survival - leafy greens and salad crops are nutritionally valuable but calorically thin. A robust plan devotes the majority of cropland to calorie and protein staples, with a diverse vegetable garden and an orchard/berry patch for vitamins, minerals, and variety.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 - Core dairy and eggs (year 1):**

- Establish **3-4 milking cows** (Jersey or Jersey-cross for butterfat and manageable size) on staggered calving, within a total cattle herd of ~6-8 head; or **15-18 dairy does** if going goat-based. Secure a bull or AI access.
- Start **~55-70 laying hens** with a 2-3-year replacement rotation.
- Benchmark: if you are consistently short of fluid milk during dry periods, add one more milker or tighten calving stagger; if drowning in milk, shift surplus to cheese/butter or reduce milkers.

**Stage 2 - Meat and staples (years 1-2):**

- Raise **~350-500 broilers/year** in batches of ~100, plus finish **~5-6 beef/dairy-beef animals/year** using the herd's own calves.
- Plant **3-5 acres of grain** and a large potato/winter-squash/dry-bean block for storable calories.
- Benchmark: track stored calories through winter; if you run short before spring, expand potato and grain acreage before adding more animals (plants yield far more calories per acre than livestock).

**Stage 3 - Diversify and secure land (years 2-3):**

- Build a **~4-10 acre vegetable garden** (biointensive if labor allows, row-crop if not) with 20-30 crops, plus an orchard/berry area.
- Secure **~60-100 acres total**, prioritizing good tillable and well-watered pasture land; degraded or dry land pushes you toward the high end.

**Thresholds that change the plan:**

- **Lower meat target (~50 lb/person):** cut beef animals to ~2-3/year and broilers to ~150-200; land can drop toward ~40-60 acres.
- **All-plant/low-dairy diet:** land need falls dramatically (toward ~0.5-1 acre/person of cropland); eliminate most pasture.
- **Poor/dry land or short season:** increase pasture and hay acreage 2-5× and add season-extension (hoop houses) for vegetables.

## Caveats

- All figures are temperate-climate planning benchmarks from agricultural extension services, USDA, and homesteading/permaculture sources; actual results vary widely with soil, climate, breed genetics, feed quality, management skill, and labor availability. First-year yields are typically below these targets.
- "Continuous" milking is a simplification: cows and goats must be bred and given dry periods; true year-round supply comes from staggering multiple animals, not from any single animal producing non-stop.
- Commercial per-cow yields (~24,000 lb) assume intensive grain feeding and management; grass-based homestead cows produce less. Homestead figures (3-4 gal/day) are more realistic for a self-sufficient operation. Likewise, the 290-egg commercial layer average reflects optimized caged/lit conditions; expect ~180-220/hen in a mixed homestead flock.
- Meat and dairy consumption figures reflect current U.S. *availability* (which overstates actual intake and includes waste); a self-sufficient farm would likely target lower, deliberately chosen levels.
- Land estimates exclude woodlot for fuel/timber (historically ~20 acres on a 60-acre colonial farm) and assume no major crop failures; prudent planning adds a buffer for both.
- USDA ERS dairy figures (651 lb milk-equivalent, ~40 lb cheese, ~6.5-6.8 lb butter) reach the public via ERS releases and trade-press reporting; exact decimals vary by cheese definition (natural cheese ~40 lb vs. all cheese ~42 lb).
